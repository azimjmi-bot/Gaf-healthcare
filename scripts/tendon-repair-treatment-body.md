## What Is Tendon Repair Surgery?

Tendons are strong bands of connective tissue that connect muscles to bones. When a muscle contracts, the tendon transmits that force to the bone, producing movement.

A tendon can be damaged by a sharp injury, sports trauma, sudden force, repetitive stress, degeneration, or an injury in which the tendon pulls away from its attachment.

**Tendon repair surgery** aims to restore the continuity and appropriate tension of the tendon so that it can heal and eventually transmit force normally again.

The exact operation varies considerably.

- A fresh finger tendon laceration may be repaired directly with specialised sutures.
- An Achilles tendon rupture may be treated through an open or minimally invasive approach in selected patients, or without surgery in others.
- A tendon pulled away from bone may require reattachment using sutures or anchors.
- A chronic or severely damaged tendon may require grafting or reconstruction rather than simple repair.
- In some longstanding injuries, a tendon transfer may be considered when the original tendon cannot adequately restore function.

This distinction is important when comparing hospitals or treatment costs in India. "Tendon repair" is not one standardised operation.

## When Is Tendon Repair Surgery Necessary?

Not every tendon injury requires surgery.

Treatment depends on the tendon, location and severity of the injury, as well as the patient's functional requirements.

Surgery may be considered when there is a complete tendon rupture, a significant traumatic tendon laceration, tendon separation from bone, significant loss of function, persistent symptoms despite appropriate non-surgical treatment, a tendon injury associated with another injury requiring surgery, a chronic injury that cannot be managed effectively with conservative treatment, a displaced or retracted tendon, or significant functional impairment in an active patient.

A complete tendon tear does not automatically mean surgery is the only treatment. Achilles tendon rupture is one example where selected patients may receive non-operative treatment with functional rehabilitation.

## When Can Tendon Injuries Be Treated Without Surgery?

Depending on the tendon and injury, non-surgical management may include rest and activity modification, immobilisation, splinting, bracing, pain management, physiotherapy, progressive strengthening, functional rehabilitation, and treatment of an underlying inflammatory or degenerative condition.

For certain Achilles tendon ruptures, functional rehabilitation can be an alternative to surgery in appropriately selected patients.

Some degenerative tendon conditions are not primarily surgical problems. Surgery may only be considered after appropriate conservative treatment has failed or where there is a significant structural injury.

## Causes of Tendon Injuries

**Sports injuries.** Sudden acceleration, jumping, falling or forceful contraction can cause tendon rupture. Common examples include Achilles, hamstring and patellar tendon injuries.

**Accidental cuts.** A deep cut to the hand, wrist or foot can directly sever a tendon. These injuries may also damage nerves, arteries, joints or bones.

**Falls and trauma.** A sudden force applied to a limb can stretch or rupture a tendon.

**Degenerative changes.** Tendons may become weaker with age or chronic overuse, making them more vulnerable to tearing.

**Repetitive activity.** Repeated loading can contribute to tendon degeneration and, in some cases, eventual tearing.

**Tendon avulsion.** A tendon can pull away from its attachment to bone. Some avulsion injuries require surgical reattachment depending on the tendon and the patient's functional requirements.

## Symptoms of a Torn or Severely Injured Tendon

Symptoms vary according to the tendon involved.

Common symptoms include sudden or persistent pain, swelling, bruising, weakness, difficulty moving the affected joint, loss of normal movement, a snapping or popping sensation at the time of injury, visible deformity in some injuries, difficulty walking or using the hand, reduced strength and tenderness over the tendon.

A complete tendon injury may cause a noticeable loss of function. A person with a significant Achilles rupture may have difficulty pushing off with the affected foot. A flexor tendon injury in the finger can prevent normal finger flexion.

## Is a Tendon Injury an Emergency?

Some tendon injuries require prompt medical assessment, particularly open injuries.

A deep cut to the hand accompanied by loss of finger movement, numbness, pale or cold fingers, severe bleeding, or suspected damage to nerves or blood vessels should be assessed in a **local emergency department**, not by WhatsApp.

Flexor tendon injuries require assessment of the skin, bones, tendon, nerves and blood supply before the definitive repair plan is established.

Do not repeatedly test a suspected tendon injury or force the joint through movement.

## Diagnosis of Tendon Injuries

Diagnosis usually begins with a detailed history and physical examination.

The orthopaedic or hand surgeon will want to understand how the injury occurred, when it happened, whether there was a cut or blunt trauma, which movement became difficult, whether there was a popping sensation, whether numbness is present, whether the patient can actively move the affected joint, and whether there are associated injuries.

**Ultrasound** can help identify tendon tears and assess tendon continuity dynamically. It can be particularly useful for selected superficial tendon injuries.

**MRI** provides detailed information about tendon structure, the extent of tearing, retraction and surrounding soft tissues.

**X-ray** generally does not show the tendon itself but can identify fractures or certain avulsion injuries and help evaluate the bones.

For suspected Achilles rupture, ultrasound and MRI can help establish the diagnosis and assess the extent of injury when clinically appropriate.

## Types of Tendon Repair Surgery

The operation is selected according to the anatomy and condition of the injured tendon.

### Primary tendon repair

The torn tendon ends are brought together and secured using specialised sutures. This is commonly used when the injury is relatively fresh and the tendon ends can be appropriately approximated.

In hand flexor tendon injuries, the strength and smoothness of the repair are particularly important because the repaired tendon must eventually glide through a confined anatomical space.

### Tendon reattachment to bone

When a tendon has pulled away from its normal attachment, the surgeon may reattach it to bone using suture anchors, bone tunnels, sutures or other fixation methods.

### Minimally invasive tendon repair

Selected tendon injuries can be treated using smaller incisions or percutaneous techniques. Suitability depends on tendon location, injury pattern, tissue quality, surgeon experience, associated injuries and patient-specific factors.

### Tendon grafting

If the original tendon cannot be repaired directly because there is a significant gap, tissue loss or chronic damage, a tendon graft may be necessary. A graft can provide a new tendon segment to bridge the damaged area. That product often sits closer to [hand reconstruction](/costs/India/Orthopedics/Hand-Reconstruction) than to a simple suture.

### Tendon reconstruction

Chronic injuries may require reconstruction rather than simple repair because prolonged injury may lead to retraction, scar formation, muscle shortening, adhesions, joint stiffness and poor tendon tissue quality.

### Tendon transfer

When the original tendon is no longer capable of performing its function, a functioning tendon may sometimes be redirected to perform another movement. Tendon transfer is different from tendon repair and is generally considered in selected severe or longstanding tendon dysfunction.

## How Long Does Tendon Repair Surgery Take?

A relatively straightforward tendon repair may take around one to two hours, while complex injuries involving multiple tendons, nerves, vessels, bone injuries or reconstruction may take considerably longer. The exact operating time should be confirmed by the treating surgeon after reviewing imaging and clinical findings.

## Recovery After Tendon Repair Surgery

Recovery is not simply the time required for the surgical wound to close. A tendon needs time to biologically heal and then gradually tolerate increasing loads.

The rehabilitation programme has to balance two competing objectives: **protect the repair from excessive force while providing enough controlled movement to reduce stiffness and adhesions.**

This is particularly important for hand flexor tendon repairs, where excessive movement can risk rupture while insufficient movement can contribute to adhesions and loss of motion.

## Physiotherapy After Tendon Repair

Rehabilitation is one of the most important parts of tendon repair treatment.

Depending on the tendon, therapy may include controlled range-of-motion exercises, splint management, progressive strengthening, scar management, tendon-gliding exercises, joint mobility exercises, functional retraining, balance and proprioception work, and sport-specific rehabilitation.

For flexor tendon repairs, rehabilitation protocols have evolved considerably, with controlled early movement used in appropriately selected patients to balance tendon healing and prevention of adhesions.

The rehabilitation programme should be prescribed by the operating surgeon and appropriate therapist rather than copied from another patient's protocol.

## What Can Affect Tendon Repair Recovery?

Recovery can be influenced by location of the tendon injury, complete versus partial tear, acute versus chronic injury, degree of tendon retraction, tissue quality, associated nerve or vascular injury, bone injury, infection, smoking, diabetes and other medical conditions, patient age, rehabilitation adherence, occupation, athletic demands and timing of treatment.

A simple tendon repair and a complex chronic tendon reconstruction should not be expected to follow the same recovery pathway.

## Risks and Complications of Tendon Repair Surgery

Potential risks include infection, bleeding, anaesthetic complications, nerve injury, blood-vessel injury, scar formation, adhesions, joint stiffness, weakness, persistent pain, tendon re-rupture, failure of the repair, delayed healing, reduced range of movement and the need for additional surgery.

Achilles tendon repair can be associated with wound complications, infection, sural nerve injury and venous thromboembolism. Sudden chest pain, breathlessness or a swollen painful calf after lower-limb tendon surgery belongs in a **local emergency department**, not on WhatsApp.

Hand tendon repair can be complicated by adhesions or rupture of the repair, making postoperative therapy particularly important. Pale or cold fingers, rapidly increasing pain, or wound drainage with fever also belong in an emergency department.

## What Happens If Tendon Repair Is Delayed?

A delay can sometimes allow tendon retraction, scar formation, muscle shortening, adhesion formation, joint stiffness and reduced tissue quality. This can make a later repair more technically difficult.

For some traumatic hand tendon injuries, early repair is preferred when clinically appropriate. In flexor tendon injuries, delayed presentation can change the surgical strategy and may ultimately require grafting or reconstruction rather than simple primary repair.

Not every tendon injury needs immediate surgery, so timing should be decided by the appropriate specialist.

These terms describe different approaches.

| --- | --- | --- |
| Tendon repair | Reconnects the patient's damaged tendon | Repairable acute tear |

In complex hand injuries, tendon grafting or staged reconstruction may be necessary when direct repair is no longer feasible.

## Open vs Minimally Invasive Tendon Repair

There is no single approach that is appropriate for every tendon injury.

Open repair allows direct visualisation and the ability to address associated injuries, and is useful in complex trauma, at the cost of a larger incision and selected wound complications.

Minimally invasive repair uses smaller incisions and may reduce some wound complications in selected procedures, but it is not suitable for every injury and associated injuries may require a more extensive approach.

The choice should be based on anatomy and evidence for the specific tendon rather than the assumption that minimally invasive surgery is automatically better.

## Can Tendon Repair Restore Normal Function?

The goal is to restore useful function, strength and movement, but a successful repair does not guarantee completely normal function.

Outcome depends on type of tendon, severity, timing, associated injuries, tissue quality, surgical technique, rehabilitation and patient factors. In hand tendon injuries, even technically successful surgery can leave stiffness or adhesions.

Desk-based work may resume relatively early if the affected limb can be protected. Manual work involving lifting, gripping, climbing or repetitive physical activity usually requires a longer recovery. Athletes require sport-specific rehabilitation.

A rehabilitation programme may progress through protection, mobility, strength, endurance, functional training, sport-specific drills and return to competition.

## Key Takeaway

**Tendon repair surgery in India is not a single standardised procedure.**

The right treatment depends on the tendon involved, whether the injury is partial or complete, how recently it occurred, the amount of tendon retraction or tissue loss, associated injuries, and the patient's functional goals.

A fresh, repairable tendon injury may be treated with primary repair. A tendon that has separated from bone may require reattachment. Chronic or severely damaged tendons may require grafting or reconstruction, while some longstanding problems are better addressed with tendon transfer.

Surgery is only one part of tendon treatment. Protection, controlled movement and progressive rehabilitation are fundamental to recovery.

For patients considering treatment in India, the most useful first step is to have the **MRI or ultrasound and previous medical records reviewed by an appropriately trained orthopaedic, hand or sports-medicine specialist** before making travel or surgical decisions.

## Medical Disclaimer

This article is intended for general educational purposes and should not replace an examination or personalised advice from a qualified orthopaedic, hand or sports-medicine surgeon. Tendon repair is not one standardised operation, and the appropriate technique, risks, rehabilitation and cost can only be determined after reviewing the patient's individual clinical and imaging information. Pale or cold fingers, severe bleeding, fever with wound drainage, sudden chest pain, breathlessness or a swollen painful calf should be assessed in a local emergency department.
