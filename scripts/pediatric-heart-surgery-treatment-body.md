## What is pediatric heart surgery?

Pediatric heart surgery refers to operations performed to correct structural or functional problems of the heart and major blood vessels in children. These conditions are often present at birth and are therefore known as congenital heart defects.

A child's heart may have an opening between its chambers, a valve that is too narrow, a blood vessel connected incorrectly, or an abnormal pathway for blood flow. Some defects place extra strain on the heart and lungs. Others reduce the amount of oxygen reaching the body.

Pediatric cardiac care is a team effort. It may involve pediatric cardiologists, congenital heart surgeons, cardiac anesthesiologists, intensivists, nurses, perfusionists, nutritionists and rehabilitation specialists. The clinical assessment, diagnosis, treatment recommendation and surgical decision remain with the treating hospital and its qualified team. GAF Healthcare coordinates records, estimates and travel. It does not perform surgery.

## Why do children need heart surgery?

Congenital heart defects develop when the heart or its major blood vessels do not form normally during pregnancy. They range from relatively small abnormalities to complex conditions that need several procedures.

The decision to operate depends on how the defect affects circulation and whether the expected benefits outweigh the risks.

**An opening in the heart.** An ASD or VSD may allow blood to flow between chambers in a way that strains the heart or lungs. Those named repairs sit on the ASD and VSD pages linked above.

**Abnormal blood vessels.** A PDA or another vascular connection may send excessive blood flow to the lungs or interfere with normal circulation.

**Insufficient oxygen in the blood.** Some defects, including certain forms of Tetralogy of Fallot, can cause cyanosis, commonly described as a bluish colour of the lips, tongue or skin.

**Narrowed valves or blood vessels.** Obstruction can make it harder for the heart to pump blood. Coarctation of the aorta is one example and has its own treatment page.

**Incorrect connections of major arteries.** Transposition of the great arteries can seriously affect oxygen delivery and may require urgent specialist care. The arterial switch page covers that operation.

**Complex heart anatomy.** Some children need staged operations to establish a circulation their heart can sustain. Glenn and Fontan pages cover two of those stages. There is no live Norwood-only treatment page.

Children may also need surgery for selected acquired valve problems or complications of previous cardiac treatment. Adult coronary bypass is a different product and sits on [CABG Surgery in India](/treatments/cabg-surgery-in-india). It is not a substitute for a pediatric congenital list.

## Warning signs of a heart problem in a child

Congenital heart defects do not always cause obvious symptoms. Some are identified during pregnancy or newborn screening. Others are discovered later during a routine examination or an investigation of unexplained symptoms.

Possible warning signs include:

- Bluish lips, tongue or skin, particularly around the mouth.
- Rapid or difficult breathing, including during feeding.
- Excessive sweating while feeding or drinking milk.
- Poor weight gain or difficulty feeding.
- Unusual tiredness during normal activity.
- Frequent respiratory infections in some children.
- Fainting, dizziness or reduced exercise tolerance in older children.
- A heart murmur detected during a medical examination.

These symptoms do not necessarily mean that a child needs surgery. They do warrant medical assessment, particularly when they are persistent or occur together.

**Seek emergency medical care** if a child develops severe breathing difficulty, sudden blue or grey lips or tongue, collapse, unresponsiveness, or other signs of serious deterioration. Do not delay emergency treatment to arrange an overseas consultation.

## Types of congenital heart defects treated in India

Pediatric cardiac centres in India treat a range of congenital heart conditions. The procedure depends on the exact anatomy and the severity of the defect. The names below are educational. They do not establish whether a particular child needs surgery.

### Atrial septal defect (ASD)

An atrial septal defect is an opening in the wall separating the heart's two upper chambers. Small ASDs may be monitored. Larger defects may increase blood flow to the lungs. US comparison is **$30,000–$80,000**.

### Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

A ventricular septal defect is an opening between the heart's two lower chambers. Some small VSDs close naturally. Larger defects can cause rapid breathing, feeding difficulty, poor growth or excessive blood flow to the lungs. ### Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

The ductus arteriosus is a normal connection before birth. When it stays open, it is a patent ductus arteriosus. A small PDA may need only monitoring. ### Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)

Tetralogy of Fallot is a combination of four heart abnormalities that can restrict blood flow to the lungs and reduce oxygen levels. ## Types of pediatric heart surgery and interventions

The term heart surgery covers several approaches. Not every procedure opens the chest, and not every child needs open-heart surgery.

![Educational illustration of open-heart, catheter and staged treatment pathways](/uploads/treatments/pediatric-heart-pathways.webp)

### Open-heart surgery

Open-heart surgery is used for many structural defects that need direct repair inside the heart. Depending on the operation, the surgeon may use a heart-lung machine to maintain circulation while the heart is temporarily stopped or isolated from normal blood flow. Examples include VSD closure, repair of Tetralogy of Fallot, an arterial switch, AVSD repair, and selected complex valve and vessel reconstructions. The incision, the use of cardiopulmonary bypass and the expected recovery vary by operation.

### Closed-heart surgery

Some procedures can be performed without opening the heart chambers or using a heart-lung machine. Examples include selected operations on blood vessels outside the heart and certain palliative procedures. Closed-heart surgery does not automatically mean a small incision or a low-risk operation.

### Catheter-based procedures

Catheter-based treatment uses thin tubes inserted through blood vessels, usually in the groin, to reach the heart or major vessels. Depending on the diagnosis, procedures may include ASD or PDA device closure, balloon dilation of selected narrowed valves, stenting of selected narrowed vessels, and other congenital cardiac interventions. These approaches may avoid a large incision. They are not suitable for every defect, and they carry procedure-specific risks.

### Minimally invasive surgery

Selected pediatric cardiac procedures may be performed through smaller incisions. Suitability depends on the defect, the child's size, the surgical team's experience and the available equipment. Minimally invasive surgery is not automatically safer for every child. Neighbouring [minimally invasive cardiac surgery](/costs/India/Cardiac-Surgery/Minimally-Invasive-Cardiac-Surgery) planning is **$8,000–$20,000** (typically **5–12 nights**) when a mini approach can honestly complete isolated work. US comparison is **$75,000–$210,000**. There is no live robotic pediatric-heart-only treatment page.

### Staged surgery for complex heart conditions

Some children need more than one procedure because the anatomy cannot be fully corrected in a single operation, or because the safer approach is staged. Families should ask the cardiac team to explain the purpose of each stage, its expected timing, potential complications and the long-term follow-up plan. Glenn and Fontan details sit on those treatment pages. Norwood and BT-shunt planning uses the cost sheets above because those treatment pages are not live.

## How is a child evaluated before heart surgery?

A treatment recommendation should be based on the child's clinical condition and the diagnostic evidence, not on the name of the defect alone.

**Medical history and examination.** The pediatric cardiologist reviews symptoms, feeding, growth, oxygen levels, previous admissions, medications and other health conditions. The examination may include heart sounds, pulses, breathing, blood pressure and oxygen saturation.

**Echocardiography.** An echocardiogram uses ultrasound to show chambers, valves, blood flow and many structural abnormalities. It is central to the assessment of most congenital heart defects. Send images or video as well as the written report.

**Electrocardiogram.** An ECG records electrical activity and can help identify rhythm abnormalities.

**Chest X-ray.** A chest X-ray may help assess heart size and the appearance of the lungs. It does not replace an echocardiogram.

**CT or cardiac MRI.** Advanced imaging may be required for blood vessels, complex anatomy, heart function or previous repairs.

**Cardiac catheterisation.** In selected children, catheterisation provides pressure and oxygen measurements or detailed anatomy. It may also be used to perform a therapeutic intervention.

**Blood tests and anaesthesia assessment.** The team may request blood counts, blood grouping, kidney function, coagulation tests or other investigations. Before surgery, the family should receive an explanation of the proposed operation, alternatives, material risks, expected recovery and the likely need for further treatment.

## When should pediatric heart surgery be performed?

There is no single correct age for all pediatric heart operations. Some defects can be monitored for years. Some need repair during infancy. Others need treatment shortly after birth.

Timing depends on the type and severity of the defect, oxygen levels and symptoms, pressure and blood flow in the heart and lungs, the child's age, weight and growth, heart function and other medical conditions, whether delay could cause irreversible complications, and the expertise available at the treating centre.

A small, uncomplicated VSD may not need immediate surgery. A newborn with a critical heart defect and poor oxygen delivery may need urgent specialist treatment. Parents should not delay a recommended procedure, and they should not arrange an elective international journey, without discussing the risks of travel and waiting with the treating team.

### What is usually included in a surgical package?

A hospital estimate may include some or all of the following:

- Preoperative investigations.
- Surgeon and anaesthesia fees.
- Operating theatre charges.
- Heart-lung machine and perfusion services, when needed.
- Standard consumables and specified implants or devices.
- Intensive care for a defined number of days.
- Ward or room accommodation for a specified duration.
- Routine medicines and postoperative tests.
- Standard inpatient monitoring.

Inclusions differ from one hospital to another. A package may exclude blood products, additional devices, extended ICU stay, complications, emergency reoperation, specialist consultations or discharge medicines. Ask for those exclusions in writing.

### What factors affect the cost?

**Complexity of the heart defect.** A straightforward closure uses a different level of resources from a complex reconstruction or a staged operation.

**Age and weight.** Newborns and very small infants may need specialised equipment, neonatal intensive care and longer monitoring.

**Hospital type.** Government, charitable and private hospitals have different pricing and funding arrangements. The bands on this page are GAF partner-hospital planning ranges, not a government tariff.

**Length of intensive care.** Ventilation, additional monitoring or treatment of complications can increase the bill.

**Devices and implants.** The procedure may require a catheter device, stent, conduit, valve or other material. The device is quoted after it is selected.

**Previous procedures.** Children who have already had surgery may need more complex investigations or a reoperation.

**Length of admission.** The total depends on how quickly the child stabilises and recovers.

**Travel and accommodation.** International families also budget for flights, visas, lodging, food, local transport and a possible extended stay. Those costs are outside the hospital band.

## What happens before, during and after surgery?

### Before surgery

The team reviews the diagnosis, investigations, medication, nutrition, infection risks and any associated medical conditions. Parents receive instructions about fasting, medicines and admission.

The surgeon and anesthesiologist explain the proposed procedure, its expected benefits, material risks and alternatives. The team should also explain whether further treatment may be needed later.

For international patients, medical records should be reviewed before travel whenever possible. The hospital may need additional imaging, examination or tests after arrival.

### During surgery

The child receives general anaesthesia and continuous monitoring. The surgeon performs the planned repair or reconstruction. Some operations require cardiopulmonary bypass. Others do not. The operating team monitors circulation, oxygenation and other vital functions. Duration varies considerably, so a single expected operating time should not be applied to all pediatric heart surgeries.

### Immediately after surgery

Many children are transferred to a pediatric cardiac intensive care unit. Some need temporary breathing support, intravenous medicines, drainage tubes or specialised monitoring. The team watches heart function, blood pressure, oxygen levels, rhythm, bleeding, kidney function and signs of infection. Not every child needs the same level or duration of support.

### Hospital recovery

As the child stabilises, the team reduces intensive support, manages pain, reviews feeding and mobility, and confirms that the repair is functioning as expected. Before discharge, parents should receive instructions on medication, wound care, feeding, activity, warning signs and follow-up appointments.

## Recovery after pediatric heart surgery

Recovery depends on the child's age, the complexity of the operation, preoperative health and whether complications occur. Some children recover relatively quickly after a straightforward procedure. Infants undergoing complex surgery may need a much longer admission and a gradual recovery at home.

**The first few weeks.** Parents may need to monitor the wound, temperature, breathing, feeding, sleep, activity and prescribed medicines. The hospital should explain which activities are permitted while the chest or incision heals.

**Feeding and growth.** Some children with significant congenital heart defects have feeding difficulty or poor weight gain before treatment. Follow the care team's recommendations for feeding, hydration and nutrition.

**Returning to school and play.** The timing of a return to school, sports and physical activity depends on the operation and the child's recovery. Follow the surgeon's instructions rather than a universal timeline.

**Long-term follow-up.** Successful surgery does not always mean that follow-up can stop. Some children need repeat echocardiograms, rhythm monitoring, medication or additional procedures as they grow. Children with complex congenital heart disease may need lifelong specialist follow-up, including the transition from pediatric to adult congenital cardiology.

**Warning signs after discharge.** Contact the treating team promptly for increasing redness or discharge from the wound, persistent fever, worsening feeding, unusual fatigue or breathing changes. Seek emergency care for severe breathing difficulty, sudden cyanosis, collapse, unresponsiveness or rapid deterioration.

## Risks and possible complications

Pediatric cardiac surgery can substantially improve circulation and quality of life for appropriately selected children, but it carries risks. The level of risk differs widely between procedures and patients.

Possible complications include bleeding or the need for a blood transfusion, infection, abnormal heart rhythms, respiratory complications, kidney injury or other organ complications, residual defects or incomplete correction, problems involving valves, blood vessels or implanted materials, neurological complications including stroke in some circumstances, the need for another catheter procedure or operation, and, in severe cases, disability or death.

These risks should be discussed in relation to the child's diagnosis and the proposed operation. General statistics cannot reliably predict an individual child's outcome. Parents should ask which complications are most relevant, how the centre manages them, and what measures are taken to reduce risk.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is pediatric heart surgery available in India?**
Yes. Pediatric cardiac centres in India perform operations and catheter-based interventions for a range of congenital heart defects. The availability of a specific procedure, and whether a centre is suitable, should be confirmed for the child's diagnosis, age, weight and clinical condition. Start with the live pediatric cardiac surgery doctor and hospital directories linked on this page.

**How much does pediatric heart surgery cost in India?**
Costs vary by procedure, hospital, age, clinical complexity and ICU requirements. GAF Healthcare planning is $3,500–$8,500 for PDA closure, $4,000–$9,500 for ASD closure, $4,500–$11,000 for VSD closure, $6,500–$16,000 for TOF repair and $12,000–$26,000 for an arterial switch. A Norwood procedure is $18,000–$40,000. Pediatric heart transplantation is $50,000–$120,000. These are partner-hospital planning bands, not current quotations. Obtain an itemised estimate from the treating hospital.

**Which congenital heart defects can be treated in India?**
Treatment is available for many conditions, including ASD, VSD, PDA, Tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, AVSD, coarctation of the aorta, TAPVC and selected complex congenital defects. The treatment plan depends on the exact anatomy and clinical condition. Dedicated treatment pages exist for ASD, VSD, PDA, TOF, arterial switch, coarctation, Glenn and Fontan. AVSD, TAPVC, Norwood and pediatric heart transplant are quoted from cost sheets because those treatment pages are not live.

**Is open-heart surgery necessary for every child with a heart defect?**
No. Some children need only regular monitoring, and selected defects can be treated with catheter-based procedures. Open-heart surgery is recommended when the anatomy and clinical circumstances make surgical repair the appropriate option.

**Can a newborn undergo heart surgery in India?**
Yes. Selected newborns undergo cardiac surgery when it is clinically necessary. The decision depends on the defect, urgency, the newborn's stability and the capabilities of the treating centre. Some conditions, including obstructed TAPVC and many arterial-switch cases, require urgent specialist care. Do not fly an unstable newborn in order to reach a planned international admission.

**How long does a child stay in hospital after heart surgery?**
The length of stay varies by procedure and recovery. PDA planning is typically 3–8 nights. ASD planning is 5–10 nights. VSD planning is 6–12 nights. TOF, Glenn and AVSD planning is often 8–16 nights. Arterial switch and TAPVC planning is 10–21 nights. A Norwood stay is planned as 3–8 weeks in a paediatric cardiac ICU. Complications can make any of these longer. Parent stay is expected.

**How long does recovery take after pediatric heart surgery?**
Recovery depends on the operation, age, overall health and complications. The treating team will advise when feeding, school, play and sports can resume. Some children need longer rehabilitation and ongoing specialist monitoring. A universal number of weeks should not be applied to every operation.

**Is pediatric heart surgery safe?**
Heart surgery carries risks, and no responsible provider can guarantee an outcome. The risk depends on the defect, the child's health, the proposed operation and the centre's experience with that operation. Ask the cardiac team for an individualised explanation of risks and how complications would be managed.

**Can children with congenital heart defects live normal lives after surgery?**
Many children benefit significantly from treatment and take part in school and age-appropriate activities. The long-term outlook depends on the original defect, the completeness of repair, heart function and any residual problems. Some children require lifelong follow-up, including adult congenital cardiology as they grow.

**Which city in India is best for pediatric heart surgery?**
There is no single best city for every child. Compare hospitals by experience with the specific defect, pediatric cardiac ICU facilities, team expertise, outcome information the centre is willing to explain, cost transparency and follow-up arrangements. Live GAF catalog cities on this page are Delhi NCR, Mumbai, Bengaluru, Chennai and Hyderabad.

**Can international patients obtain a treatment estimate before travelling to India?**
Yes. Hospitals may provide a preliminary estimate after reviewing medical records. The final plan and cost can change after examination, additional investigations or a change in the child's condition. Share the echocardiogram images, not only the written report.

**What medical records should parents send for a second opinion?**
Useful records include the latest echocardiogram report and images, diagnosis, age, weight, oxygen saturation if available, medication list, previous operation notes, discharge summaries and relevant test results. The receiving specialist may request cardiac catheter, CT or MRI studies as well.

**Can pediatric heart surgery be performed without a large incision?**
Some congenital defects can be treated through catheter-based procedures or selected minimally invasive operations. These methods are not suitable for every child. The safest option depends on the anatomy and the clinical objective, not on incision size alone.

**Will my child need another operation in the future?**
Some children need only one repair. Others need further procedures because of residual defects, growth, valve or vessel problems, or a planned staged pathway such as Glenn followed by Fontan. The surgeon should explain the likely long-term plan before travel.
